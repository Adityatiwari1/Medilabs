# The Hidden Cost of Cheap Lab Glassware: A Procurement Manager's Guide

*Why the lowest quote on your glassware RFQ is rarely the lowest total cost.*

A lab procurement manager gets three quotes for 500 volumetric flasks. One vendor comes in 30% below the other two. On paper, that's an easy win. Six months later, the same manager is filing replacement orders for flasks that failed calibration checks, cracked during autoclaving, or arrived with graduation lines too faint to read accurately.

This is the part of lab glassware procurement nobody puts in the RFQ: the cheapest quote and the cheapest *total cost* are almost never the same number.

## Where the real cost hides

Glassware pricing looks simple: cost per unit times quantity. But three variables sit underneath that number, and none of them show up on a supplier's price sheet.

**Breakage rate.** Borosilicate 3.3 glass resists thermal shock far better than soda-lime glass, but not all "borosilicate" glassware is manufactured to the same annealing standard. Poorly annealed glass carries internal stress that shows up as breakage under autoclave cycling, months after purchase. A batch with a 4% breakage rate versus a 1% rate changes your real cost per usable unit by more than the original price gap.

**Tolerance drift.** Class A and Class B glassware carry different accuracy tolerances for a reason. If your lab specifies Class A for HPLC prep or pharmacopoeial assays and receives glassware that is nominally Class A but poorly calibrated, every result downstream of that flask inherits the error. Nobody notices until an audit or a failed reproducibility check traces back to the glassware itself.

**Documentation gaps.** A batch certificate of conformance is not paperwork for its own sake. It is what lets your lab pass a regulatory audit without re-verifying every flask in inventory. Suppliers who cut price by skipping batch documentation are moving that verification cost onto you, later, at a worse time.

## A quick framework for evaluating quotes

Before comparing unit price, ask four questions of every vendor:

- Is the glass borosilicate 3.3, and can they cite the annealing standard?
- Does the quoted accuracy class match what your protocol requires, not just what's cheapest to produce?
- Is a batch certificate of conformance included, or a separate line item?
- What is their typical breakage/defect rate on delivery, and will they document it?

If a vendor can't answer these directly, the quote isn't comparable to one that can. ## Catalogue pricing vs custom quotation

Many labs default to catalogue pricing because it's fast. But catalogue pricing is built for average order profiles, not your lab's specific volume, class requirements, or delivery timeline. A custom quotation, once you know your real specification (accuracy class, documentation level, order volume, delivery schedule), frequently comes in lower per-unit than catalogue pricing for orders above a few hundred units, because it strips out margin built for one-off buyers. The tradeoff and when each approach makes sense is covered in more detail [here](https://medilabexports.com/blog/laboratory-supply-catalogue-vs-custom-quotation).

## The takeaway for procurement

Treat glassware quotes the way you'd treat any capital equipment purchase: total cost of ownership, not sticker price. A flask that survives 3 years of autoclave cycles and holds its calibration is cheaper than a flask that needs replacing at 18 months, even if the second one cost 20% less upfront.
